For every problem there used to be 10 great alternatives. Today, it's over 100.

These were likely built over the weekend and some would break during their first real project. A patch would generally probably fix it but then a few more iterations would see the app dead as real users didn't really care about the fancy dashboard you created.

For all we can say, that's just plain noise hidden under the pretext of **Vibe Coding**.

I've been playing around with Claude Code quite seriously for the past eight months. Along the way, my interest in building products found its way into my work. Today I help build software on a day-to-day basis.

So this isn't an outsider's voice. I like to vibe code, but for one reason only: to test an idea. To see if it holds up in real use cases before I commit to it.

That's the only distinction that matters now. Not whether you *can* build it. Whether it's worth building at all. Because except for some cases, where it lacks context and data, AI is developing faster than a new enginner is learning.

---
## The old playbook

There are a few proven ways to find ideas.

- **Scratching your own itch.** The tasks you hate. The daily friction. It's the best candidate for a vibe-coded tool, and the most likely to end with you as its only user.
- **Unbundle that big software.** One tool does fifty things. You wish it did one of them properly.
- **Rebuild old tools with AI.** Plenty of tools predate AI. You could build a cheaper, smarter alternative.

Don't get me wrong. Most successful software companies started on exactly these premises.

But that playbook assumed building was hard. Look around, my mom if she put her will to it, could probably spin up an app today.

When anyone can build in an evening, everyone scratches the same itch at the same time.

So what's left?

---
## The slow hunch

I recently saw a TED talk by Steven Johnson from 2010.

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0af00UcTO-c&t=543s

I won't summarise it. Watch it if coming up with ideas and building them is something you care about. But one idea stuck with me.

Steven calls it *the slow hunch*. Most breakthroughs aren't sudden flashes. Darwin's theory of natural selection didn't arrive in a moment. It incubated for years.

That's the scarce thing now. Not speed. Time spent with a problem.

AI can generate a hundred tools in a week. It can't sit with a problem for a year. That's where ideas worth building come from.

---
## Let hunches collide

Hunches don't grow in isolation. They get better when they run into other people's half-ideas.

> [!note]
> So, dear managers: give people space to think. Maybe the technology doesn't exist yet. Maybe it's the confidence that's missing. Either way, incubation time is how good ideas survive. If your organisation allows for it, you're lucky. Mine does. :p

And keep ideas open. Don't protect them. Let them get challenged.

That's what open source does best. It lets ideas collide in public.

---
## Build fast, commit slow

So build the side projects. In the office. Outside it.

Then sit on them. For days. For months.

Talk about them in the open. Make a fool of yourself explaining the idea. You might get dismissed. But you don't need permission to build anymore.

Software is permission less.

If an idea hits the right chord, it becomes the leverage you needed.

Before you call it a product, ask three questions:

1. Would you still care about this in three months?
2. Does someone other than you feel this pain?
3. Does it survive being explained out loud to someone who might laugh?

If the answers are yes, keep going.

Vibe coding didn't remove the hard parts of building software. It removed the easy parts, which used to hide the hard ones.

Typing was never the bottleneck. Judgment was. Now judgment is the only thing left.

So don't rush. Prototype in an evening. Commit over months.

**Judgment is the one thing you still do better than the machine.**
